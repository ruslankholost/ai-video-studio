# 11 Business and clients

A client does not pay for skill in one model. A client pays for a finished marketing product that brings reach and sales. This stage covers what to sell and to whom, how to price, how to set up a deal (brief, estimate, prepayment, corrections, rights, NDA), where to find the first clients, and how to get the first money in 30 days.

All income claims and figures in the sources are self-reports of speakers and members and are not independently checked. The legal and tax material in the sources follows Russian law. It does not apply to you directly, so check contracts, taxes and copyright locally or with a lawyer before signing anything. This document gives no price list: ask for current market rates, and fix your own after 3 real projects with a known cost.

## Input and output

Input:
- `brief.md` (from the client or built with them) and `idea.md`; for a brand with a ready technical task, that task as an appendix to the contract.
- Finished work from the project for the case: the video, the edit, and `publish.md` with results (views, requests, sales).
- The user's decision on direction and niche (principles 1-4 and 13).

Output:
- One-line offer, a list of services with prices and packages, a one-page proposal.
- Deal terms: brief, estimate, contract, prepayment, number of corrections, deadlines, rights to the content (the seven things of principle 41).
- Cases (task, what was done, how long, result) and a portfolio page (stage 10).
- A 30-day first-money plan and a list of contacts.

Client folder (our proposal): `client/offer.md`, `client/proposal.md`, `client/brief.md`, `client/contract-terms.md`. Prompt wording for these documents is in `templates/client-docs.md`.

## Principles

### What clients buy

1. **A client needs a ready marketing product, not "skill in one video model".** An entrepreneur can do simple things alone, so your edge is volume, speed and result.
2. **You earn because you know your audience, not because you know a tool.** The client wants to see what reach you gave, what scripts you wrote, what ideas you built. Models change every month; understanding the client stays with you.
3. **Three questions before work:** for whom (the client and their viewer: age, pains), why (sales, requests, recognition), how to attract (where the viewer is and what to show).
4. **The idea must be yours (the agent writes script and prompts).** Content "for yourself" (flying dragons, cartoons without meaning) is not what a client needs.
5. **Clients order what is cheaper than classic production.** One AI video costs 70-90 percent less (a claim of the source). Instead of one video in weeks you can have 10+ variants for tests in days, by 1-2 people.
6. **What clients look for:** AI UGC; AI video creator; visuals for brands and products; AI advertising. Demand claims: AI video up several-fold in a year, business AI integration and images also up. Video is the main entry point.
7. **Look for the first clients in small and medium business,** which adopts AI fastest. Do not limit yourself to your own country.
8. **Sell not "cheaper" but more variants for the same money.**
9. **Sell the result, not videos.** Give a guarantee only after a successful case. The main argument is the price of a follower compared to ads. When the talk is about followers, there are fewer corrections: "the client looks at numbers, not at whether the nose in the frame came out right".
10. **A large visible project brings orders and consultations, not direct money from views.**
11. **Trust and budgets come from skill and reputation, not luck.** Learn, show work, help others, and orders start to come.
12. **The market ahead (a forecast of the source):** value shifts to idea, script and taste. "Everyone will work with AI, few will work well." Systems win: a subscription to your service, agents instead of one-off generations.

13. **Choose one direction and one niche; do not study all 20.** Focus for a month.

15. **UGC and AI creators:** a live creator became expensive, and AI solves that. One workflow works for UGC and for AI influencers: a fashion video and a product integration, in five filming styles (selfie, lavalier, get-ready-with-me, unboxing, in motion). One product photo can give 8 videos with 8 characters aimed at the audience, each with its own ad text, hook and reveal. Speech with clean pronunciation, readable label text and decent sound make UGC usable in more languages.
16. **Real estate:** take what already worked and adapt it to the client. A video for an expensive house can be made without a drone or a trip, from listing photos. Aerial shots without a drone come from `nano_banana_pro`; animation from a first frame and a last frame in `seedance_2_5`. For a hook, catch the audience of a specific city (open with a panorama and the city name).
17. **Real estate, one realtor's series:** a format for one goal (subscription): historical time-lapses of the city; a series of 4-5 videos a month; one video reached six figures of views; two case posts brought 7 clients before payment. (Self-report.)
18. **Infographics and product cards:** a designer's lecture on infographic trends, ideas and building a complex card (generation, background, elements, typography, color correction). Aim: make a product stand out, show benefits, build information hierarchy. Sell a set: a main card and 5-7 infographic slides. Start by redoing 3 cards of real products.
19. **Fashion product shoots from a brand brief:** a shoot must tell a story and create desire. Concept: take an element of a luxurious life and weave it into the visual. A prompt is a system. Fix a result by changing the original prompt, not the image, or quality is lost. Flow: brief, idea and references, character, angles, a series of frames.
20. **AI bloggers:** a non-existent character who runs an account, gains followers and brings money. Three ways: brand advertising; an expert with their own product; content for businesses by order. Risk: the account can be blocked in a platform cleanup; proof (screenshots, screen recordings) stays with you.
21. **Brand series and micro-dramas:** brands buy a "serial story" for reach and traffic; foreign platforms buy micro-dramas (episodes of 2-2.5 minutes, cut-off on the climax). The micro-series market is claimed to grow several-fold by 2031.
22. **Integrations:** a brand pays for a place in the story. Money for integrations needs an audience. The source claims a cartoon is better for a brand than realism.
23. **A cartoon for a clinic:** the preparations themselves became heroes, ordinary boxes turned into living characters. The approach is universal, any objects work. In the prompt for a box character keep the packaging design, keep the label readable, and keep "one single body".
24. **Early paid work:** animating still photos of a shoot for a marketplace card. The client saves a separate video shoot. At low prices you need automation to make a video in half an hour; for marketplaces such videos often come as a gift with a collection shoot.
25. **Western freelance directions** (a course on editing): a SaaS launch video; a talking head with graphics; hyper motion of a product; a 3D fly-through of real estate; a 2D explainer; an editorial explainer. A video costs roughly 1-1.6 generations in credits; plan 2-3 attempts. On a mid-size order generations are about 1 percent of the check.
26. **Editing as a service:** editing of a lesson and a quality reel are priced per piece; a motion package of a brand (a brand book for videos, subtitle templates, lower thirds, endings) sells for more than one video. One client, one brand folder, and each next video is faster and cheaper.

### Pricing, packages, subscription

27-28. **Price lists from the sources** differ by an order of magnitude because of different products and markets. We keep no price list. Price ranges in the sources include per-video, per-carousel, strategy, a turnkey AI system and monthly account management.
29. **The ladder:** services, retainers, products, teaching, agency. Each level funds the next; start from the bottom.
30. **Price from the market plus generations.** Competitors vary widely ("the market is raw"). The first month one author priced low and then raised. A one-off video "for fun" costs 5 times more, because reference cards for one video are not worth building.
31. **Three tiers of one author:** a demo; a middle tier of 4-5 videos a month; a "for result" tier with a follower guarantee. Ceiling: on the middle tier one person handles 4-5 clients, at the limit 6-7; after that, assistants or a team.
32. **Subscription "content factory":** Start (about 30 videos a month, one product, format test); Growth (about 90 a month, 3 a day, hook tests); Scale (10+ a day, several accounts and products). Sample monthly package: 12 Reels, 20 photos and 8 carousels. Regular income every month instead of one-off orders; you can take about 10 brands.
33. **Editing packages are sold per month, not per piece:** Start (8 videos: subtitles, cuts, music); Standard (16 videos plus motion graphics and covers); Premium (turnkey support: structure, scripts, editing, design, report).
34. **Move the client to a support package.** Specialists often make one video and vanish, though they could make ten.
35. **Do not name a price apologetically.** Three packages (basic, standard, premium); "they compare your packages, not the market". Never "price on request" (claimed to lose most clients). Do not dump prices and do not work for credits.
36. **How to raise prices:** after every three successful clients raise by a fifth to a third; through value (add a service and raise); scarcity ("I take at most 3 clients a month"); three packages; a case with numbers as a reason.
37. **How to stand out:** specialization ("AI for beauticians" beats "AI for everyone"), speed ("ready in 48 hours"), process (behind the scenes builds trust), guarantee (a free correction, but no more than 2 iterations within the brief).
38. **Digital products and teaching are separate lines:** prompt pack, guide, templates, mini-course, a club on subscription; teaching: an intensive, a group course, mentoring, corporate. Teaching comes only after real cases.
39. **Four work models:** your own agency; subcontract (an agency sells, you do the AI part); in-house; your own teaching. You can switch; a beginner is more comfortable starting in a team.
40. **Level by self-test (5 yes/no questions):** one character in 10 frames without distortion; a turnkey video in 3 days; 5 cases in one niche; at least one regular client; more than half of income from repeat orders. 0-1 beginner, 2-3 middle, 4-5 pro. Level is set by skill and income together, not by years or number of courses.

### Contract, prepayment, corrections, NDA, rights

41. **Fix seven things before any project:** brief, estimate, contract, prepayment, number of corrections, deadlines, rights to the content. Brief and proposal templates take about 15 minutes with the agent (`templates/client-docs.md`).
42. **Prepayment.** The sources split between 100 percent (practice of a production company) and 50 percent with the rest after delivery (a typical contract; "at least 50 percent as a standard for authors"). Default: 50 percent at the start, the rest on delivery. (default; the user may choose 100 percent)
43. **Generation costs are always paid by the client.** With a small budget, warn the client in advance that there will be a couple of generations and you do not answer for the result.
44. **Discuss the idea and script first, then build the estimate.** The estimate follows your experience of credit use. Put search and redoing into the estimate: searching costs money.
45. **Corrections:** the contract states the number of corrections and the stage; extra corrections are paid by the client; two free iterations is the recommendation. A character can be redone from scratch even after approval, which is fine if the contract covers it: in one real case the client rejected an aged look after approval, the character and all scenes were redone, and the extra generations were billed to the client.
46. **A good brief reduces redoing.** The technical task is an appendix to the contract, not a separate document ("no technical task means the client changes the task endlessly"). Sign before work starts.
47. **A typical contract has eight parts:** subject; deadlines; corrections (2 free); payment (prepayment, the rest on delivery); rights to the result (license or assignment; assignment costs 30-50 percent more); NDA; liability (limited to the cost of the work, penalties for delay both ways, force majeure as its own clause); termination. Practice: fix the exchange rate if the price is in another currency; fix the delivery format (MP4, PNG or HTML by email); an electronic signature is legal where the law allows it. This structure comes from a Russian-law lecture, so adapt it to your country.
48. **Copyright (Russian-law lecture, adapt locally):** a human is the author; AI cannot be one. If you wrote the prompt, picked and refined the result, you are the author. The client gets a right to use, not to own. A full transfer of rights is a separate clause with an extra fee. Transfer rights to a specific character, not to all works and prompts (prompts are your intellectual property). Without a contract all rights stay with the author. In the US and the EU, work without substantial human contribution is not protected, so record your contribution (prompts, choices, refinement, editing).
49. **Kinds of rights transfer:** non-exclusive license (base price); exclusive (+30-50 percent); full assignment (the sources say +30-50 or +50-100 percent; take the lower range as a start and adjust).
50. **NDA** is needed when the client is a company or a public person, you see their inner kitchen, work with content before publication, or create characters or avatars for a brand. Not needed for a simple one-off content, open publication, no trade secret. Content of an NDA: a concrete subject, a term (2-3 years; unlimited is a red flag), proportional penalties (capped at the contract value), exceptions, mutual obligations.
51. **NDA and portfolio:** add "the contractor may use the visual result in the portfolio without naming the client"; anonymize; describe without showing ("AI content for a fashion brand, 50 units a month, engagement up 40 percent"); ask separate permission; discuss the portfolio before the NDA is signed.
52. **If your work is stolen:** record the original (a notarized screenshot, a web archive, an email to yourself); a pre-trial claim ("delete within 7 days or sign a license"); platform complaints (removals in 24-72 hours); court. In advance: a watermark on previews, metadata, keep source files and correspondence, an authorship clause in the contract.
53. **Payments and taxes** depend on country and legal form. The source covers Russia only; ask an accountant where you live. Never take card-to-card payments without documents. For foreign clients use a payment service that works in your country.
54. **Private and corporate clients:** private ones decide fast, flexible terms, card payment, contract optional; corporate ones are long-term, NDA required, a contract with a company, payment by invoice, a brief and acts.

### First money and where to find orders

55. **Start with what you already know:** do not invent a new road, strengthen the one you walk. Take a field where you have a year or two, write down the common pain of clients and close it with AI visuals (not necessarily video).
56. **First contacts through your close circle and demos on other people's faces.** Say honestly "I am learning, let me make you a photo", for a symbolic sum or a barter. Write personally by name. Make it realistic, with a twist, no "hyperspace".
57. **Path to the first client in 4 steps:** one direction and niche (offer); a portfolio of 5-7 works without a client; where to find clients; deal rules. Portfolio without a client: 1-2 spec ads for real brands "as if", a redo of someone's weak ad, 2-3 works for acquaintances free for a review, one video with a plot (directing). First 2-3 projects can be cheaper in exchange for a review and the right to show the work.
58. **Where to get clients:** cold messages, 20 a day, each with a ready frame for the addressee's brand (the main channel for the start); acquaintances who are entrepreneurs; freelance marketplaces; your own content (Reels with process and before/after); subcontract from agencies. How to write: not "Hello, I am Anna, an AI creator, here are my works", but go into the account, see a gap, think of the effect, generate a frame or a short video and send it with an idea.
59. **Where else:** messenger chats, freelance marketplaces, zero-commission networks, a professional network (B2B), your own blog, cold outreach, meetups and conferences (one talk can bring 5-10 requests), portfolio networks; a threads-style feed can be the best first touch because the distance to the reader is small, while a messenger channel is more the end of a funnel. Many freelance clients are repeat clients. Method "10 letters": find 10 experts or brands, study their blog, write "I see [problem]. Here is how I can help: [solution]. Here is my case: [link]".
60. **You need hundreds of attempts, not ten.** "Ten mailings without answer are not yet an attempt." (This contradicts "10 letters = 1-2 clients"; take the harder, newer rule: 20 cold messages a day, 100 a week.)
61. **Package the case at once** (screens, numbers, proof) and show it everywhere. Update the numbers in comments. Two posts with a case brought 7 clients.
62. **Do not wait for the perfect moment.** Seven mistakes that slow income: shallow work with 10 tools; no portfolio packaging; naming the price apologetically; no prepayment; endless corrections; no client base; waiting for "when I am ready".
63. **One action to raise the check if you already earn:** a subscription, a new direction, or delegation. Do it within a month and count the result. For a pro: raise the check through script and direction, move clients to subscription, systematize with the agent, delegate routine.
64. **Beginner to middle:** one niche, 5 cases, 3 first paid projects, even at a symbolic price.

### What vacancies ask for

65. **Pattern from a feed of about 20 job posts:** nearly all demand is for people who make AI video from references and a script, with a portfolio. Most ask for video and animation, many ask for a portfolio or examples in the response, some name short vertical formats, some name specific models, a few allow beginners. Pay is stated in a minority; the rest say "to be discussed".
66. **Stated pay varied widely:** from a full-time monthly salary in a game studio to small starting pay with training. Some clients pay generation costs for a pool of work.
67. **A response asks for:** a portfolio, examples, the AI pipeline, deadlines and price, experience with voice tools, expectations on pay, real cases. A test task appears: a short video review of an AI tool.
68. **Before responding, build the portfolio** (stage 10). A response to a vacancy means agreeing to the client's terms: "how much to pay is decided by the client".
69. **Typical order formats:** ad videos and cartoons; story videos up to 10 a month; Reels for a dessert brand (scripts, actors, filming, editing, real shots with AI graphics); an AI avatar and Instagram promotion for a housing complex; editing an animated video with voice-over; an agent skill to automate Reels editing; a 3D video about yachts; jewelry design from references; an expert speaker or a presenter.

### 30-day first-money plan

70. **Plan 1 (if you are starting):** week 1 choose direction and niche, assemble the offer; week 2 make 3 cases on any terms; week 3 make 2-3 more cases and package the portfolio and profile; week 4 send 100 personal messages, aiming at the first well-paid project. The source's goal is a modest first sum.
71. **Plan 2:** days 1-3 choose a niche and one tool; days 4-7 portfolio (a channel or notes page, 3 cases, service descriptions); days 8-14 three free projects, get a review; days 15-21 outreach to 10 potential clients with an offer and a case; days 22-30 the first payment, 1-2 clients, a review, raise the price. One path: one tool used every day for two weeks, 3 free projects, a portfolio of 5 cases, 10 people written to, a first paid order for a few hours of work, the client returned. Homework: one way to monetize; a niche; a price (3 services, 3 packages); write to 3 people today.
72. **Count the economics of models before the first payment.** "On expensive models you will exceed the budget, and a monthly subscription is hard to justify to a client."

### General working rules

73. **Learn through mistakes:** understand what a prompt is made of instead of copying it; a mistake is good ("tokens burned, find where it broke"); each model has its own job; count the economics; if you did not ask, you did not understand.
74. **Team and roles:** the best quality comes from narrow specialists working together; solo, you need adjacent skills (camera, light and color). "A good creator must either know or know where to ask." Slow, careful generation is where everyone begins; generating well and with meaning is the level up.
75. **The three pillars:** fast, good, cheap. You can choose two.
76. **One person cannot carry everyone.** Mind the client ceiling of principle 31; offer clients a team or assistants.
77. **Study by sections** ("Earning", "Video generation", "Image generation"), not everything in a row; use the community chat for support; for cinematic videos start with preproduction, production and postproduction (stages 00-08 of this repo).

## Defaults (resolved for this studio; the user may change them)

- Prepayment: 50 percent at start, the rest on delivery. (default; the user may change it)
- Rights: non-exclusive license for a specific character; full assignment only as a separate clause with an extra fee. Prompts stay the author's.
- Early work: up to 3 free or symbolic-price projects for a review, with a generation budget cap fixed in advance and approved by the user before the first paid generation.
- Legal text: no template contract is shipped. Before the first client, the user passes the contract, taxes and NDA points through a local check. Treat the contract structure of principle 47 as a checklist, not a legal text.
- Price list: not published until 3 real projects with known cost exist.
- Client texts contain no invented numbers, reviews or names: cases and proposals use only real data with a source.
- These five items (prepayment, price list, rights surcharge, free projects, legal check) are decided by the user when the first client appears. Before the first client conversation the agent walks through them again with the user.

## Workflow

1. Choose one way of monetizing and one niche (principle 13). Write the offer by the formula "I make [product] for [niche] so that [result]". **User's ok.**
2. Write the price: three services and three packages (basic, standard, premium), no "price on request" (principles 27-36). **User's ok.**
3. Build a portfolio without a client: 1-2 spec ads, a redo of a weak ad, 2-3 works for acquaintances for a review, one video with a plot. Present each as a case (principle 57, stage 10).
4. Make a brief, a one-page proposal and the deal terms (`templates/client-docs.md`).
5. Write to clients: 20 cold messages a day, each with a ready frame for the addressee's brand; acquaintances personally; vacancy responses with a portfolio (principles 55-60, 65-69).
6. Before starting a project fix the seven things (principle 41) and get the prepayment (principle 42). **User's ok.**
7. For a brand with a brief: the brief as an appendix to the contract; analysis of the product, concept, visual elements, technical implementation, a series of frames (principle 19).
8. Run the project through stages 00-08, approving characters before expensive generations. Corrections follow the contract; extras are paid by the client. Every paid generation still needs the user's explicit yes on the exact list.
9. Deliver in the agreed format; ask for a review; write the case (task, what was done, how long, result); raise the price by the three-client rule.
10. Move the client to a subscription (content factory, editing package, support) (principles 32-34).

## Common mistakes

- Seven mistakes that slow income (principle 62).
- Without clients vs with clients: endless tool study (with clients you study what the order needs); waiting for a perfect portfolio (show 5 cases with a result); "I do everything" (one niche and a clear offer); dumping and working for credits (take prepayment and hold the price); waiting for clients to come (write to clients every day).
- "A mess in the head": learned this and that and nowhere got established; studying all 20 directions; a portfolio of mixed works; "flying dragons" for the ego; one video and gone; repeating 30 times what nobody cares about; an offer "for everyone"; a cold "Hello, I am Anna"; a portfolio in a shared drive; judging level by years and courses.
- Copying prompts without understanding them; expensive models without counting the economics; a copy-paste mailing; a guarantee without a successful case; a messenger as the first touch.
- A small budget without warning the client; a contract without corrections; not putting search and redoing in the estimate.
- Portfolio: 50 works, "I know 15 tools", "price on request", no niche, "made it and forgot it".
- An AI-blogger account can be blocked; check a look for censorship in advance, because some models refuse explicit looks.
- Endless corrections without a limit; card-to-card payment without documents.
- An unlimited NDA and penalties out of proportion (a very large fine on a small project is not adequate).
